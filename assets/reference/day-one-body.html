The First 24 Hours

Day One

Your last drink is behind you. Here's what your body actually starts doing over the next 24 hours — and why the first day can feel so rough while it's healing.

0–3 Hours

Your blood alcohol starts falling

Blood alcohol falling

With no more alcohol coming in, your blood alcohol only goes down. Your liver breaks it down at a roughly fixed pace — on the order of one standard drink per hour — and nothing (coffee, water, a cold shower) can speed that up. You simply have to give it time.

0–12 Hours

Your liver works overtime

In the liver
Alcohol Acetaldehyde Acetate

Your liver converts alcohol first into acetaldehyde — a toxic compound — and then into harmless acetate. While that acetaldehyde is being cleared, it drives much of the nausea, flushing and generally "poisoned" feeling of a hangover. Over the day, the backlog clears.

6–24 Hours

Your brain rebalances

Nervous system
GABA · calmingGlutamate · excitatory

Alcohol had been boosting your brain's calming signal (GABA) and dampening its excitatory one (glutamate). With the alcohol gone, the brain is briefly left over-excited until it recalibrates — which is why day one can bring anxiety, irritability, a racing mind, tremors and broken sleep.

12–24 Hours

Your heart runs a little hot

Heart & blood pressure elevated

That same over-excited nervous system revs up your "fight-or-flight" response, so heart rate and blood pressure often run higher than usual on the first day — sometimes alongside sweating and a jittery, "wired" feeling. For most people this settles as the days pass.

Across the day

You start rehydrating

Hydration refilling

Alcohol suppresses vasopressin, the hormone that tells your kidneys to hold on to water — so you've been running dehydrated. As you take in fluids today, that reverses, easing the pounding dehydration headache and dry mouth.

Across the day

Your blood sugar steadies

Blood sugar steadying

A liver busy processing alcohol does a poorer job of releasing stored glucose, so your blood sugar swings — part of the shakiness and fatigue. On day one it begins to level out, especially once you're eating regular meals again.

Please read this

When day one is a medical matter

For someone who has been drinking heavily and daily, suddenly stopping can trigger dangerous withdrawal — including seizures and a life-threatening state called delirium tremens — most often in the first 1–3 days.

Warning signs include severe shaking, confusion, hallucinations, a racing heart or fever. If that sounds like you or someone you're helping, don't detox alone — contact a doctor or emergency services. Medically supervised withdrawal is safe, routine, and far more comfortable.
